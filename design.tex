\section{Fault-tolerant migration protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\OurImplementation{} makes migration failure-atomic and efficient by integrating it with
replication rather than running it beside it.
For failure atomicity, it moves each group $G_i$ as a transaction whose progress is recorded by
four entries in the replicated logs of the donor and the recipient shard groups
(Table~\ref{tab:log-entries}), so a newly elected leader can tell from its own log how far each
group got and roll it forward.
For efficiency, the data never goes through the log: the donor leader writes the blocks of $G_i$
straight into the recipient leader with one-sided RDMA, and the recipient leader replicates them
to its followers along a chain (Fig.~\ref{fig:migration_flow}).
We first state the failure model (Section~\ref{sec:fault-model}) and give an overview of the
protocol (Section~\ref{sec:migration-protocol}).
We then follow one group through the phases of a migration (Section~\ref{sec:migration-phases}),
the index update at the recipient (Section~\ref{sec:index_updating}), and the way clients are
served while it is in progress (Section~\ref{sec:serving-during-migration}).
Finally we describe how the protocol handles failures (Section~\ref{sec:failure-handling}) and
how it builds and repairs the chain (Section~\ref{sec:chain-replication}).

\subsection{Failure model}
\label{sec:applicability}
\label{sec:fault-model}

\OurImplementation{}'s fault-tolerant migration protocol targets in-memory key-value stores.
We consider a KVS that (i) partitions the key space into logical units such as shards or slots, (ii) replicates each partition across a set of replicas using a leader-based replication protocol~\cite{raft,paxos-simple,chain-replication} with synchronous writes, and (iii) runs on replicas that fail by crashing (\textcolor{blue}{\emph{fail-stop}}).

We call the replicas of a partition a \emph{shard group}. One replica acts as the leader and the others as followers. The leader appends writes and other commands to the group's \emph{replicated log}, and an entry is committed only after it has been durably stored by a majority of the shard group.
A crashed replica loses its in-memory state, but can rejoin later a shard group as a new member and obtain its state from the other replicas through the replication protocol.
Failed replicas are eventually detected by the others, e.g., through timeouts or broken connections.
A shard group of $2f+1$ can survice at most $f$ failures. This ensures that every committed update, and the data that is being migrated, survives on at least one replica.

An elasticity operation involves two shard groups, a \emph{donor} shard group that currently owns the data being migrated, and a \emph{recipient} shard group that will assume ownership.
Migration proceeds concurrently with client requests.
